## Market Narrative

The peace dividend arrived, and Wall Street cashed it. Stocks gapped higher at the open and held the bid into the close on Monday, June 15, after President Trump declared late Sunday that a deal to end U.S.-Iran hostilities was "now complete," with an official signing ceremony slated for Friday in Switzerland. The single sentence that mattered most to traders was not diplomatic at all — "Ships of the World, start your engines. Let the oil flow!" — because it signaled the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne crude transits. The war premium that had been bleeding into energy prices, freight rates, and equity risk appetite for two weeks reversed in a single overnight session.

The mechanics of the move were textbook risk-on. Crude oil collapsed more than 5%, dragging the entire inflation-expectations complex lower with it. That, in turn, did the heavy lifting for equities: lower oil means lower headline inflation pressure, which means less reason for the Federal Reserve to stay restrictive, which means a friendlier discount rate for long-duration growth stocks. The Nasdaq Composite's 2.38% surge — nearly double the S&P 500's 1.49% gain and twice the Dow's 1.20% — tells you exactly where the relief flowed: into the mega-cap technology names and the rate-sensitive growth complex that had been the prime casualties of the early-June war scare and the June 4 chip-stock rout. The VIX, which had been camped near 18 through the conflict, dropped more than 8% to settle in the mid-16s, a clean signal that the market repriced the geopolitical tail risk as substantially resolved rather than merely paused.

Breadth was constructive but notably narrower than Friday's session. The Russell 2000's relatively muted 0.79% gain against the Nasdaq's 2.38% marks a reversal of last week's rotation: where Friday saw small caps and equal-weight sectors lead while mega-caps lagged, Monday was a mega-cap-led tape. This is the signature of a "risk-premium unwind" rather than a "broad economic acceleration" — money rushed back into the highest-beta, most-shorted, longest-duration assets first, exactly as you would expect when a binary geopolitical overhang lifts. The quality of the rally will be tested later in the week, when the macro calendar — not the news wire — takes the wheel.

And that is the central tension of this tape. Monday was a one-factor day: peace. But the rest of the week is a different animal entirely. This is "Fed Focus Week." Kevin Warsh chairs his first FOMC meeting, with the rate decision and fresh economic projections landing Wednesday afternoon, sandwiched against a critical May retail sales print the same morning and Accenture and Kroger earnings Thursday. The market that celebrated cheaper oil on Monday must now reckon with an inflation rate still running at 4.2% and a brand-new Fed chair who has signaled he wants to talk less, guide less, and possibly lean more hawkish than the Powell-era consensus. The peace premium gave bulls a head start; the FOMC will decide whether they keep it.

## Sector & Theme Breakdown

**Mega-cap technology** led from the front. Microsoft rose 1.95%, Apple gained 1.77%, and the broader cohort participated in proportion to its rate sensitivity. The read-through is straightforward: with oil down 5% and the geopolitical risk premium collapsing, the names with the longest cash-flow duration got the biggest tailwind from the implied easing in financial conditions. Tesla was a laggard within the group at +0.84%, consistent with its more idiosyncratic, demand-cycle-driven trading pattern rather than a pure duration play.

**AI software and platforms** were the percentage leaders. Palantir jumped 4.69%, outpacing the hardware names and signaling that when the macro overhang lifts, the most expensive, highest-multiple software names get re-rated fastest because they carry the most embedded rate risk. This is the same dynamic in reverse that punished the group during the June 4 selloff, when the Nasdaq fell 4% for its worst day since April 2025 as traders fled chip stocks.

**Semiconductors** rebounded as part of the broad tech bid. The chip complex had been the epicenter of the early-month pain — first the June 4 rout, then the war-driven volatility — so Monday's recovery represents a partial repair of an oversold, heavily de-risked group rather than fresh leadership. Watch whether the rebound holds through the FOMC: semis are simultaneously the highest-beta expression of risk appetite and acutely sensitive to any hawkish repricing of the rate path.

**Space** continued to be dominated by the SpaceX (SPCX) story (see Focus Deep Dive). The newly public name extended its historic debut, and the sympathy complex — the smaller launch and lunar names — traded with elevated correlation to it, as a freshly minted index-flow magnet reshapes the entire sub-sector's liquidity map in its first full week of trading.

**Energy** was the day's structural loser, and deservedly so. With WTI down more than 5%, the integrated majors and the E&P complex gave back the war premium that had inflated them through early June. The transmission is direct: lower crude compresses near-term cash-flow estimates for producers. But the second-order effect is bullish for the broad market — energy is a cost line for almost every other sector, and a sustained move lower in oil is a tax cut for consumers and industrials alike.

## Macro & Economic Data

**Empire State Manufacturing Index (June):** The New York Fed's gauge came in at 5.7, a soft but still-positive reading that points to a manufacturing sector that is expanding only marginally. A single-digit print this shallow signals momentum that is barely above stall speed — not a contraction, but not the V-shaped factory recovery that bulls had hoped tariff reshoring would deliver. For the Fed, a tepid manufacturing pulse is mildly disinflationary at the margin and gives the doves a talking point, but it is nowhere near weak enough to force the issue against a 4.2% inflation backdrop.

**Industrial Production (May):** Released alongside the regional survey, the production data rounds out a picture of an industrial economy treading water rather than accelerating. The combination of a thin Empire State reading and lukewarm output keeps the "soft landing, slow grind" framework intact and argues against any narrative that the economy is either overheating or rolling over.

**CPI (May, released June 10 — still the dominant data point in the tape):** Headline consumer prices rose 0.5% month-over-month, pushing the annual rate to 4.2%. Crucially, the core reading was far better behaved — up just 0.2% month-over-month and 2.9% year-over-year — with energy responsible for more than 60% of the monthly headline gain. This is the single most important nuance for this week: the inflation problem is concentrated in energy, and energy is exactly what just collapsed 5% on the Iran deal. If Monday's oil move holds, the mechanical arithmetic of the next two CPI prints improves materially, because the energy component that drove the headline overshoot will deflate. That is the bull case in one sentence — and the reason equities could look through a 4.2% print.

**Rates:** The 10-year Treasury yield, which had hovered near 4.47% on Friday after shedding roughly 10 basis points in the prior session, drifted in the 4.4% area as the risk-on, lower-oil backdrop pulled inflation breakevens down even as the equity rally argued for higher real yields — a tug-of-war that left nominal yields roughly stable. The 2-year sat near 4.15%, keeping the 2s10s spread modestly positive around +25 to +30 basis points. The structurally important signal remains intact from last week: with the 2-year trading above the 3.75% upper bound of the funds-rate target, the bond market is still pricing the Fed as more likely to hold or hike than to cut in the near term — a direct contradiction of the political pressure for lower rates. ## Fed, Rates & Credit

The entire week orbits Wednesday's FOMC decision — Kevin Warsh's first as chair. The market is essentially unanimous on the mechanical outcome: CME FedWatch shows roughly 97% odds of no change, leaving the target range near 3.75% at the top. The decision, therefore, is not about the number; it is about the framing.

Three things will move markets. First, the dot plot and economic projections: with inflation at 4.2% and core sticky near 3%, the risk is that the Summary of Economic Projections removes the easing bias entirely and shifts to a neutral — or even modestly hawkish — stance, formally acknowledging that cuts have been pushed out. Second, Warsh's tone. He has signaled a preference for a leaner Fed that communicates less and steps back from the granular forward guidance markets grew dependent on under Powell. A chair who deliberately withholds hand-holding introduces a new, structural source of rate-path uncertainty — and uncertainty, all else equal, demands a higher term premium. Third, any acknowledgment of the oil collapse: if Warsh explicitly cites lower energy prices as improving the inflation outlook, that is a dovish tell that would extend Monday's rally; if he focuses on the stickiness of core services and the 4.2% headline, that is the hawkish path that would put the week's equity gains at risk.

The credit and rates backdrop frames the stakes. The bond market has spent weeks pricing yields biased higher "until something breaks," and the persistence of long-end yields near cycle highs despite genuine progress toward peace is the tell that the inflation and fiscal/independence concerns are structural, not headline-driven. Gold's continued climb to $4,357.70 — rising on a risk-on day, which is unusual — reinforces the read that part of the bid is a hedge against Fed-independence erosion and currency debasement rather than pure geopolitical fear. When gold rallies as war risk falls, the market is telling you the metal is trading on monetary policy, not on missiles.

## Geopolitical & Policy

The event of the day, and the prime mover, was the U.S.-Iran framework. The reported 14-point draft envisions a final settlement on the nuclear file within 60 days, with Trump indicating that any final agreement would cap Iranian uranium enrichment at levels that "could never be used by the military." Pakistan's prime minister flagged a formal signing ceremony for Friday in Switzerland.

The transmission chain to markets is clean and worth drawing in full: **deal announced → Strait of Hormuz reopening signaled → seaborne crude supply-risk premium evaporates → oil falls ~5% → headline inflation expectations decline → real and breakeven yields adjust → Fed easing path looks less constrained → equity risk premium compresses → highest-duration assets (mega-cap tech, AI software) rally hardest.** Every link in that chain played out on Monday in the correct order and the correct magnitude, which is itself a confidence signal that the move was a genuine repricing rather than a thin-liquidity squeeze.

The crucial caveat — and the reason this is a framework, not a fact — is execution risk. An earlier ceasefire in this same conflict collapsed within weeks. Until Friday's signing is on paper and the first tankers actually clear Hormuz, the deal remains a market-priced expectation, not a settled outcome. A signing-ceremony breakdown or a verification dispute over the 60-day nuclear timeline would re-arm the entire war-premium trade in reverse. Position sizing into Friday's event — against a market that is now leaning long on peace — is the discipline question of the week.
