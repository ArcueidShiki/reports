## Market Narrative

Thursday was the day the market chose its story. Offered two competing headlines — the United States bombing roughly 90 targets inside Iran overnight, and reports that SK Hynix's $26.5 billion Nasdaq listing was more than seven times oversubscribed — Wall Street looked straight past the missiles and bought the memory chips. The Nasdaq Composite jumped 1.30% to 26,206.89, the Nasdaq 100 rose 1.44% to 29,673.74, the S&P 500 added 0.81% to 7,543.64, and even the lagging Dow tacked on 139.02 points to 52,487.41. The Russell 2000 quietly put in one of the day's better performances, up 1.22% to 2,992.55, as falling oil and easing yields gave small caps room to breathe. Semiconductor stocks as a group surged about 4%, their best day since the Samsung-triggered rout on Tuesday, and the sector's leadership was unmistakably about one thing: the AI-memory trade got a $26.5 billion vote of confidence overnight.

The geopolitical backdrop, by any normal standard, should have been terrifying. Hours after President Trump declared the Iran ceasefire "over," CENTCOM announced it had "started conducting additional strikes against Iran to further degrade their ability to threaten freedom of navigation in the Strait of Hormuz" — the second consecutive day of US strikes, hitting approximately 90 targets. Tehran retaliated by firing missiles and drones at US assets in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and Jordan, according to Iranian state media. And yet crude oil — the one instrument that translates Middle East risk into portfolio P&L — spiked early and then fell. WTI settled down about 2% at $72.08 and Brent lost 2.2% to $76.30, unwinding a large chunk of Wednesday's 4–5% surge. The market's read was coldly mechanical: the strikes were aimed at degrading Iran's *capacity* to close the Strait, actual oil flows were not disrupted, and a war premium that was manufactured by podium headlines deflated the moment the tape decided the escalation was contained. Falling oil pulled the 10-year Treasury yield down about 3 basis points to 4.543%, and that combination — crude down, yields down — was the permission slip equities needed.

Beneath the risk-on surface, however, Thursday delivered the most important *macro* data point of the week from an unlikely source: a soda company. PepsiCo, reporting fiscal Q2 before the open, beat on revenue ($24.18 billion vs. $23.96 billion expected) but missed by a penny on core EPS ($2.20 vs. $2.21), and — far more importantly — told investors that tightening consumer budgets are now visibly slowing US food and beverage demand. North America beverage volumes fell 4%, North American organic revenue shrank 0.5%, and management leaned on promotions and discounting hard enough to compress core operating margins by 40 basis points. The stock fell 3.82% to close near $137 — by one account its worst single day since May 2022 — and the options market saw a rush into puts. On a day when the weekly jobless claims printed a solid 215,000, Pepsi's warning is the clearest evidence yet of the two-speed economy: labor still firm, but the inflation-squeezed consumer trading down in real time.

So the market enters Friday with a split personality. The AI-capex machine — memory, wafers, networking — is being showered with capital: SK Hynix's ADR pricing tonight, Micron pledging up to $3 billion for the US wafer supply chain plus a headline plan to lift its US plant spending ambitions to $250 billion, and sell-side price targets on memory names racing higher. Meanwhile the consumer staples aisle is flashing late-cycle stress, and the Fed's hike-versus-hold debate (September odds still hovering around a coin flip) sits unresolved in the middle. Thursday's tape said the AI trade wins that argument — for now. Friday's SKHY debut and Delta's pre-open earnings will say whether it keeps winning.

The 2026 pattern is now unmistakable: the AI trade's center of gravity has migrated from GPU compute to the memory/storage bottleneck, and NVDA has become the funding leg on days the memory complex rips.

**Energy.** The majors gave back part of their two-day war-premium windfall as crude reversed: WTI −2% to $72.08, Brent −2.2% to $76.30. The sector remains hostage to the podium — armed on a sentence, faded on a strike assessment — and Thursday's message was that traders will not pay for a premium the physical market refuses to validate.

**Commodities & crypto.** Gold futures rebounded roughly 1% to the ~$4,125 area (after Wednesday's slide to ~$4,030, the lowest since July 2), catching a modest safe-haven bid on the Gulf missile exchanges. ## Macro & Economic Data

**Initial jobless claims (week ending July 4): 215,000 — consensus 218,000, prior 217,000 (revised).** The four-week moving average eased 3,750 to 218,750. Continuing claims rose 8,000 to 1,814,000 (consensus ~1,820,000), the highest since late March. Interpretation: the labor market remains in its now-familiar "low firing, slow hiring" equilibrium. Initial claims in the mid-210s are historically tight and give the Fed zero urgency to ease; but continuing claims grinding to four-month highs says re-employment is getting slower — the unemployed are staying unemployed longer. That mix argues for patience, not panic, at the Fed: it does not veto a September hike (the labor floor is holding), but it does weaken the case that the economy is running hot. Rates barely moved on the print; the day's bond bid came from oil, not from claims.

**The de facto macro release: PepsiCo's consumer commentary.** Formal data was thin on Thursday, which elevated Pepsi's earnings call to macro-event status. Management attributed the 4% drop in North America beverage volumes and flat convenient-foods volumes to "tightening consumer budgets" under inflationary pressure, and guided to *higher input cost inflation in the second half*. Read through the Fed lens: this is mild stagflation-at-the-margin evidence — softer real demand coexisting with sticky-to-rising input costs. It supports neither the clean soft-landing story (demand is eroding) nor the clean hike story (real consumption is weakening). It is exactly the mix that keeps the FOMC split 9-hike/8-hold/1-cut, as the June dots showed.

**Rates & Fed pricing.** The 10-year yield slipped about 3 basis points to 4.543%; the 2-year sat near 4.13% and the 30-year just under 5% (~4.97%), leaving 2s10s around +41 bps. CME FedWatch-implied odds of a September hike remain roughly a coin flip (~50–55%, versus ~66% before the soft June payrolls report). The bond market treated Thursday as an oil-unwind day, not a data day.

## Geopolitical & Policy

**Event:** Second consecutive day of US strikes on Iran — approximately 90 targets — explicitly framed by CENTCOM as degrading Iran's ability to threaten Strait of Hormuz shipping. Iran retaliated with missile and drone fire at US assets in Bahrain, Kuwait, Qatar and Jordan (per Iranian state media). No confirmed disruption to oil flows; no US casualties reported by the close.

**Transmission mechanism:** Escalation headlines → oil spikes early Asia/Europe → traders assess that (a) strikes target Iran's *anti-shipping capacity* rather than oil infrastructure, and (b) tankers keep moving → war premium unwinds → WTI −2% to $72.08, Brent −2.2% to $76.30 → breakeven inflation expectations ease → 10Y yield −3 bps → rate-sensitive equities and small caps outperform → VIX drifts to 16.67. The full chain ran in a single session, in the *opposite* direction of Wednesday.

**The asymmetry to respect:** this pricing embeds a benign assumption — that Iran cannot or will not actually close the Strait. Roughly a fifth of global seaborne crude transits Hormuz. If a tanker is hit *after* two days of "capability-degrading" strikes, the market must reprice not just the premium but the credibility of the degradation thesis, and the move would be violent. Oil at $72–76 is priced for containment; it is not priced for a mistake. Separately, the USMCA non-renewal review continues to simmer with barely any market pricing — the slow-burn tariff front remains the most under-owned macro risk into the fall.

### Fundamental Context

The oversubscription is a referendum result: two days after Samsung's record profit was sold for −5.5% (proof the *positioning* was crowded), institutions just bid 7x for the purest HBM asset on earth (proof the *thesis* is intact). SK Hynix holds roughly 50–70% of the high-bandwidth-memory market; Wall Street expects HBM shortage conditions to persist through 2027; and the proceeds fund the Yongin fab and advanced packaging — capacity that is largely pre-sold. Micron's same-day moves — a 10-year wafer supply lock-in with GlobalWafers (raw-input security, the same "contract-locker" logic as Apple–Broadcom) and up to $3 billion now inside a stated ambition of $250 billion of US plant spending — say the memory makers themselves believe the cycle has years, not quarters, to run. The bear case is unchanged and unbothered: this is exactly what peak-cycle capex euphoria has looked like in every prior memory cycle, and the Fed has now formally named AI-infrastructure demand as an inflation source, meaning the trade funds itself into higher rates. Friday's SKHY open is the cleanest single print of whether public-market demand matches the book: a strong first trade validates the "structural shortage" narrative; a broken deal at the largest-ever ADR listing would be the sell-the-news signal of the year for the entire complex.
