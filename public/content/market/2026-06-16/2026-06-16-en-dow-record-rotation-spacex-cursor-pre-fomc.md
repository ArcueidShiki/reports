## Market Narrative

The day after the party, the bill arrives — and on Tuesday the market started counting its money very differently than it had on Monday. Twenty‑four hours after the U.S.–Iran peace framework detonated a broad, mega‑cap‑led risk rally, Wall Street did something subtle but important: it kept buying, but it bought entirely different things. The Dow Jones Industrial Average climbed another 345.54 points, or 0.67%, to 52,016.57 — its second consecutive record close — while the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.15% and the S&P 500 slipped 0.55%. The Russell 2000 gave back a fractional 0.17%. This is the anatomy of a rotation, not a retreat. Money did not leave the building; it walked out of the technology wing and into the industrial, financial, and value corners that anchor the price‑weighted Dow.

The proximate driver was a violent reversal in semiconductors. After three sessions in which chip stocks had ripped higher — first on the June 12 breadth thrust, then on Monday's peace‑premium unwind — institutions rang the register hard. Intel fell roughly 7%, Marvell dropped 7.3%, AMD shed 5.9%, and Micron handed back 5.1% of its multi‑day surge. When the most crowded, highest‑beta trade in the market gets sold while the index barely moves, you are watching disciplined profit‑taking ahead of an event, not a sentiment break.

And the event is the entire reason Tuesday traded the way it did. Wednesday afternoon brings the most consequential Federal Reserve meeting in years: Kevin Warsh's first decision as Chair, accompanied by a fresh Summary of Economic Projections and his debut press conference. The market is functionally certain on the mechanical outcome — CME FedWatch put the odds of no change near 97%, leaving the target range at 3.50%–3.75% — which means Tuesday was never about positioning for the number. It was about lightening up on the assets that carry the most rate risk before a brand‑new chair, with a reputation for hawkish instincts and a stated preference for talking less, takes the podium. Selling your highest‑duration winners the day before that kind of binary is not fear. It is housekeeping.

Underneath the index‑level calm, the macro tape delivered one genuinely jarring data point. May housing starts collapsed 15.4% month‑over‑month to a 1.177 million annualized pace — the weakest reading since May 2020, the depths of the pandemic shutdown. The juxtaposition — a record on the Dow, a collapse in groundbreaking, and a VIX melting to 15.86 — captures the strange equilibrium of this tape: complacent on volatility, rotational on leadership, and utterly hostage to what Warsh says on Wednesday.

The single most electric story of the session, however, had nothing to do with the Fed and everything to do with the most talked‑about new listing on the tape. ## Macro & Economic Data

**Housing Starts (May):** The headline was a shock — privately‑owned housing starts plunged 15.4% to a 1,177,000 annualized rate, down 8.7% from a year earlier and the lowest level since May 2020. A drop of this magnitude in a single month is rarely about fundamentals alone; it points to builders abruptly pulling back on breaking new ground, most plausibly a combination of still‑elevated mortgage rates (tethered to a 10‑year yield stuck at 4.47%), softening buyer traffic, and margin discipline after a long stretch of incentive‑heavy selling. The read‑through to the broader economy is genuinely two‑sided: residential construction is a leading cyclical indicator, so a collapse this steep is a yellow flag for second‑half growth and a quiet argument for the doves — but it is also disinflationary at the margin (less construction demand for materials and labor), which marginally helps the Fed's inflation math even as it complicates the growth picture.

**Building Permits (May):** Crucially, the forward‑looking series held far steadier — permits came in at a 1,413,000 annualized rate, down just 0.7% from April's revised 1,423,000, with single‑family authorizations actually rising 0.6% to 886,000. This divergence is the single most important nuance in the housing data: starts cratered while permits barely budged. That gap argues the May starts collapse was more about timing, weather, and short‑term builder caution than a structural demand cliff — if permits (the pipeline) are stable, the starts number should partially mean‑revert. Markets read it exactly this way, which is why a pandemic‑low starts print did nothing to dent a record Dow.

**Import Prices (May):** Import prices rose 1.0% month‑over‑month, decelerating sharply from the prior 1.9% pace. The direction matters more than the level here: the moderation in imported inflation, layered on top of the energy collapse, reinforces the disinflationary undertow building beneath the still‑hot 4.2% headline CPI. The pipeline pressure that had worried the bond market through early June is easing at the margin.

**CPI (May, released June 10 — still the dominant macro anchor):** Headline consumer prices rose 0.5% on the month, holding the annual rate at 4.2%, but the core read was far better behaved at +0.2% month‑over‑month and 2.9% year‑over‑year, with energy responsible for more than 60% of the monthly headline gain. This remains the crux of the entire week's setup: the inflation problem is concentrated in energy, and energy just fell for a second straight session. If Monday and Tuesday's oil declines hold, the arithmetic of the next two CPI prints improves mechanically as the energy component that drove the overshoot deflates. That is precisely why equities can look through a 4.2% print — and precisely what Warsh must decide whether to validate or resist on Wednesday.

That is why Tuesday's pre‑emptive de‑risking of the highest‑duration tech names was rational: the distribution of Wednesday outcomes is wider than usual precisely because the man delivering them is an unknown quantity at the podium.

Third, whether Warsh acknowledges the oil collapse. If he explicitly cites falling energy prices as improving the inflation outlook, that is a dovish tell that would reward the cyclical rotation and potentially re‑ignite tech. If he anchors on the 4.2% headline and the stickiness of core services, that is the hawkish path that would validate Tuesday's defensive posture and pressure the growth complex further. Gold sitting essentially flat at $4,356.62 — neither confirming nor denying the inflation‑hedge bid — and a VIX melting to 15.86 tell you the market is not hedged for a hawkish surprise.
