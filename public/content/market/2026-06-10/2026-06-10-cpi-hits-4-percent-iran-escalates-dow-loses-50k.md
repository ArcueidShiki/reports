## Market Narrative

Wednesday delivered the most consequential macro session of the month: the May Consumer Price Index printed a 4.2% annual rate — the highest in three years and the first 4-handle since April 2023 — while the US-Iran conflict escalated from "tense ceasefire" to active tit-for-tat strikes. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 952.90 points (−1.87%) to 49,918.78, decisively losing the psychologically important 50,000 level. The S&P 500 dropped 1.61% to 7,266.99, logging its 13th 1% drawdown of 2026, and the Nasdaq Composite fell 1.98% to 25,169.50. The VIX jumped nearly 12% to 22.22, its highest close in weeks.

The day's structure mattered as much as its magnitude. Stocks opened lower after the 8:30 AM CPI release and overnight news that Iran had downed a US Army Apache helicopter near the Strait of Hormuz, prompting retaliatory American strikes. Remarkably, breadth was initially constructive — at mid-morning 62.7% of US issues were advancing and 230 of 503 S&P constituents were green, suggesting rotation rather than liquidation. That changed around noon, when President Trump said the US is "going to be attacking them very hard" and would not rule out targeting Iranian bridges and power plants. The bid evaporated. Sellers controlled the tape into the close, every attempted bounce failed, and the major averages finished at fresh intraday lows — a distribution pattern, not a panic flush.

The deeper problem for equities is that the two shocks are the same shock. The Iran conflict, now in its fourth month, has pushed WTI crude to $90.03 (+2.07% today) and Brent to $93.10, and that energy impulse is now fully visible in the inflation data: energy CPI rose 3.9% in May alone and is up 23.5% year-over-year, with gasoline up 7.0% on the month. Headline CPI at 4.2% is now running above the federal funds rate (3.50%–3.75%) for the second consecutive month — a negative real-policy-rate configuration that historically forces central banks to act. CME FedWatch now prices roughly 70% odds of a rate *hike* by year-end, a stunning regime inversion from the rate-cut consensus that prevailed this spring.

After the close, the escalation continued on both fronts: the US launched a fresh round of strikes on Iranian positions, and Oracle — the AI-infrastructure bellwether — reported blowout fiscal Q4 results but saw its shares drop 7–10% in extended trading after disclosing plans to raise an additional $20 billion in equity and debt to fund its AI buildout. The market's message was uncharacteristically clear: in a 4% inflation world with rising rates, even record backlogs don't excuse capital hunger.

## Macro & Economic Data

**May CPI (the day's main event).** Headline +0.5% MoM, exactly in line with consensus (prior +0.6%); YoY +4.2%, in line with consensus but up sharply from April's 3.8% and the hottest since April 2023. Core CPI +0.2% MoM, *below* the +0.3% estimate (prior +0.4%); core YoY 2.9% vs 2.9% expected and 2.8% prior. Components: energy +3.9% MoM (+23.5% YoY), gasoline +7.0% MoM, food +0.2%, housing +0.2%, owners' equivalent rent +0.3%, new vehicles −0.3%, used cars −2.0% YoY-trend continuing, apparel +4.8% YoY, transportation services +4.1% YoY, medical care services +3.6% YoY. Core goods actually *fell* 0.1% on the month — tariff pass-through remains muted.

The interpretation is two-sided and the market chose the bearish side. The benign read: virtually all of the acceleration is energy, core momentum actually cooled (two-month core run-rate annualizes near 3.0–3.6% and May alone near 2.4%), and shelter at +3.4% YoY continues its disinflation. The hawkish read — the one rates markets adopted: headline is what households see, energy shocks of 6–12+ months duration embed expectations, core has still drifted up from 2.8% to 2.9% (+0.4pt since the war began), and a Fed with a 3.50%–3.75% policy rate is now ~50–70bp *below* headline inflation. Negative real policy rates during a supply shock is the 1970s playbook, and the new Fed leadership knows it. FedWatch moved to ~96.5% odds of a hold at the June 16–17 meeting, with hike odds by year-end around 70%.

**May Treasury budget statement.** The deficit printed $293 billion; adjusted for calendar shifts it rose $71 billion (+32%) YoY. The detail that mattered: customs duty *refunds* of $21.97B essentially canceled gross customs collections of $21.93B — net customs flow of −$42 million for the month, gutting the tariffs-as-revenue narrative and adding a fiscal wrinkle to the inflation story.

## Geopolitical & Policy

**Event chain:** Iran downed a US Apache helicopter near the Strait of Hormuz (Tuesday night) → US launched retaliatory strikes on Iranian targets → Iran's Revolutionary Guard hit US bases in Jordan, Bahrain and Kuwait → Trump declared Iran "will have to pay the price," said the US would attack "very hard," and floated seizing Iran's "oil infrastructure points" including Kharg Island → after Wednesday's close, the US struck Iran again.

**Transmission mechanism:** Hormuz risk premium → crude (WTI $90, Brent $93) → headline CPI (energy +23.5% YoY) → negative real policy rate → hike repricing → multiple compression in long-duration growth equities + dollar strength → gold paradoxically *falls* because the shock is inflationary rather than growth-destroying, so real yields rise. The second-order channel runs through the consumer: Walmart's CEO publicly warned that fuel prices have become a "stress point" for US customers — the energy tax is already visible to the largest retailer in America. The longer Hormuz flows are impaired (realistically months, not weeks), the more the shock migrates from headline into core via transport, freight and petrochemical channels.

### Fundamental Context
Oracle's quarter was objectively excellent — 21% revenue growth at this scale, OCI +93%, a $638B backlog up 363%. The problem is the *quality* of how that backlog converts: $55.7B of FY26 capex, margin pressure guided to continue, and now $20B of fresh external financing in a 4.5% 10-year world. The bull case says RPO is contractual and prepaid GPU deals de-risk it; the bear case says AI infrastructure has become a capital-markets-dependent flywheel exactly when capital is repricing higher. Both can be true. The stock's reaction tells you which one the marginal buyer believes at a ~41x market CAPE with household equity allocation at a record ~51% of financial assets — the concentration stat of the day, higher than the 2000 peak of ~45%.
