## Market Narrative

Friday was the quiet, jittery end to a loud, ugly week. On the surface almost nothing happened — the S&P 500 finished a hair lower at 7,354.02 (−0.05%), the Dow shed 44.51 points (−0.09%), and the Nasdaq Composite slipped 60.98 points (−0.24%) to log its fifth consecutive losing session. Beneath that placid headline sat one of the most revealing tape days of the quarter: the index that mattered least to the average headline (the Russell 2000) hung onto the 3,000 level it first conquered Monday, defensives and biotech ripped, and the Magnificent Seven bled out one more time. This was not a market falling apart. It was a market changing leadership.

Two forces collided into the close. The first was macro: at 8:30 a.m. ET the Bureau of Economic Analysis reported that headline PCE inflation — the Fed's preferred gauge — climbed to 4.1% year-over-year in May, the highest reading since April 2023 and the first print above 4% in this cycle's re-acceleration. Core PCE firmed to 3.4%, the hottest since October 2023. The numbers landed roughly in line with consensus on the annual figures and even a touch soft on the month-over-month headline, which is the only reason equities did not simply fall out of bed. But a "4-handle" on PCE in front of a Federal Reserve chaired by Kevin Warsh — who has already pulled forward guidance and let the dot plot drift toward a hike — is a genuinely uncomfortable backdrop, and the bond market knows it.

The second force was sentiment, and it was all about artificial intelligence. Overnight, The New York Times reported that OpenAI is leaning toward delaying its blockbuster IPO until 2027 rather than listing this autumn, after watching SpaceX's June debut round-trip from euphoria back to its offer price. That single headline crystallized a week-long unease: that AI valuations had run too far ahead of monetizable cash flow, that the cost of the buildout (memory, power, data centers) is now showing up in consumer prices, and that the marginal buyer of the most expensive AI names may be getting tired. Asia got there first and violently — South Korea's Kospi tripped a circuit breaker after an 8% intraday plunge and closed down 5.8% — and the tremor rolled west into the US mega-cap complex.

Yet the most important thing about Friday is what did *not* break. Roughly two-thirds of S&P 500 members advanced through the session. Utilities (+0.84%) and Financials (+0.37%) led; Moderna, Eli Lilly, Acadia and a basket of biotech names posted high-single to double-digit gains; ServiceNow and FactSet rallied. The pain was concentrated almost surgically in semiconductors, AI infrastructure and the priciest secular-growth franchises. The "everything rally" of early 2026 has become a "something rally," and right now the something is value, defensives, small caps and healthcare — not silicon. For a tape that spent the week obsessing over whether the AI trade is cracking, the quiet resilience of the median stock was the real signal.

## Sector & Theme Breakdown

**Semiconductors / AI hardware — the epicenter.** Despite Micron's blowout earnings two days earlier, chips could not hold a bid. Nvidia (NVDA) fell 1.64% to $192.53 — now roughly 18% below its May 14 closing high of $235.47 — and ticked up only marginally to $192.79 after hours. AMD dropped 2.6%, Intel slid ~2.6% to about $129, Arm Holdings (ARM) fell 4%, and Marvell (MRVL) lost 3.4%. Micron itself gave back about 2.1% after its 15.7% earnings pop on Thursday, opening down 4.5% premarket near $1,159 before stabilizing. The single ugliest chip print was ON Semiconductor (ON), which cratered roughly 23% to about $91 after announcing a $7 billion acquisition of Synaptics — the market's verdict on a large, debt-financed deal struck into a tape that no longer rewards aggressive empire-building.

**AI infrastructure & power.** The selling spread one layer down the stack. Bloom Energy (BE) fell ~12% to about $272, and Western Digital (WDC) dropped ~10.5% — both prior darlings of the "AI needs power and storage" thesis, both now subject to profit-taking as the data-center cost narrative flips from tailwind to scrutiny. Teradyne (TER) lost ~10%. The throughline: anything whose valuation was underwritten by an uninterrupted AI capex ramp got re-priced for the possibility that the ramp slows or that its costs compress everyone's margins.

**Mega-cap tech.** The drag actually began Thursday, when Apple (AAPL) fell 6.2% after announcing price increases on MacBook and iPad models, and Microsoft (MSFT) dropped 3.5% after hiking Xbox prices — both explicitly citing higher chip and component costs. That is the memory super-cycle showing up where it hurts: in the income statement of the companies that buy the memory. Friday the Magnificent Seven basket (MAGS) traded flat-to-lower, a relief only relative to the rest of the week.

**Defensives & value — the new leadership.** Utilities and Financials were the only two S&P sectors solidly green. Healthcare was the day's star at the single-stock level: Moderna (MRNA) surged ~11.9% to about $67.56 as the S&P's top performer following its investor day (including an in-vivo CAR-T program for autoimmune disease); Eli Lilly (LLY) jumped 6.65% to $1,202.68; Acadia Pharmaceuticals (ACAD) climbed ~12% on European approval of Daybue for Rett syndrome. SELLAS Life Sciences (SLS) squeezed ~14%. The rotation is not subtle: capital is leaving high-multiple AI and finding homes in cash-generative defensives and catalyst-driven biotech.

**Space & satellite.** A bright spot inside "new tech": AST SpaceMobile (ASTS) rallied ~11% to about $73, and SpaceX (SPCX) — the most-watched recent IPO — clawed up ~1.5% intraday from a premarket dip near $151 on anticipation of its fast-tracked addition to the Russell 1000 after the close (with a Nasdaq-100 add slated for July 6) and news of a planned Starlink consumer mobile push targeting Verizon, AT&T and T-Mobile. The space complex is trading on index-inclusion mechanics and product roadmap, decoupled for now from the AI-valuation angst.

**Energy.** Soft, in sympathy with crude. The sector fell ~0.41% as WTI dropped another 3.8%. The irony of the day: Iran reportedly struck a cargo vessel near the Strait of Hormuz, yet oil *fell* — because tankers kept flowing and the supply-disruption premium continued to bleed out of the curve.

## Macro & Economic Data

**PCE Price Index (May, released 6/26):** This was the headline event. Headline PCE rose 0.4% month-over-month (matching April) and 4.1% year-over-year — above 4% for the first time this cycle and the highest since April 2023. Core PCE rose 0.3% m/m (matching April) and 3.4% y/y, the highest since October 2023. The annual readings were essentially in line with the Dow Jones consensus, and the monthly headline came in a touch below the hottest whispers, which let risk assets — and gold — exhale slightly. But the directional message is unambiguous: the Fed's preferred inflation gauge is re-accelerating, not cooling, and it is doing so while the consumer remains strong.

**Personal income & spending (May):** Personal income jumped 0.7% (versus roughly flat in April and a ~0.4% consensus), and personal spending also rose 0.7% (up from 0.4% in April). The personal savings rate held at 3.0%. Translation: the US consumer is still spending freely into rising prices. That is excellent news for near-term growth and corporate revenue — and terrible news for anyone hoping sticky demand would force the Fed to ease. It hands a hawkish chair every reason to keep the hike option live.

**Labor (weekly):** Initial jobless claims for the week ending June 20 fell 12,000 to 215,000, with the four-week average at 224,250 — still historically low. Continuing claims rose 21,000 to 1,821,000. The labor market is not cracking; layoffs remain muted even as continuing claims drift up at the margin.

**Durable goods (May):** Orders fell 4.5%, but April was revised up to +8.5% (from +7.9%). The headline drop is largely payback for a volatile, aircraft-driven April surge rather than evidence of a capex collapse.

**Growth (Q1 2026, third estimate):** Real GDP was revised up to +2.1%, from the +1.6% second estimate. The upward revision reinforces the "resilient economy" read and undercuts any recession narrative — again, supportive of growth but unhelpful for the rate-cut case.

The composite macro picture is the uncomfortable one for duration and for long-duration equities: above-target, re-accelerating inflation; a strong consumer; a solid labor market; and firmer growth. That is not stagflation — growth is fine — but it is "no-landing-with-sticky-inflation," and it is precisely the regime in which a Warsh Fed can justify sitting tight or even tightening, and in which the most expensive equities carry the most valuation risk.

Guidance was even louder: Q4 FY26 revenue of a record **$50.0B ± $1.0B**, gross margin ~86.0%, and EPS of **~$31.00 ± $1.00**. The driver is the AI memory super-cycle — surging demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) alongside DRAM and NAND, and, critically, surging *prices* for anything memory-related. As TheStreet Pro's Stephen Guilfoyle framed it, "there are basically two stories… growing demand for high bandwidth memory as well as traditional Flash and DRAM… [and] surging prices for anything having to do with memory or storage." The stock reaction is the lesson. MU surged 15.7% Thursday and lifted the SOX 3.2% intraday — then gave it all back into Friday, opening down 4.5% premarket and closing off ~2.1%. When a historic beat-and-raise cannot hold its gains for 48 hours, the tape is telling you sentiment, positioning and the macro discount rate now matter more than the fundamental print. The memory cycle is real; the willingness to pay up for it is what's wobbling.

### BlackBerry (BB) — reported 6/26

A quieter beat. Fiscal Q1 2027 net income more than quadrupled and revenue rose 25.6% to $152.9 million on QNX automotive-software momentum. Shares slipped ~3% premarket after a near-20% surge the prior session — the same "good print, exhausted buyers" pattern playing out in miniature.

### Nike (NKE) — reports Tuesday 6/30 (preview)

The next consumer bellwether. Consensus looks for revenue down ~3% to about $10.85B and EPS of $0.12 (versus $0.14 a year ago). Options imply a ~8.5% post-earnings move, below Nike's ~12% average over the prior four quarters. Stifel cut its target to $50 (Hold), saying it is "not ready to call a bottom." Read-through risk for the whole consumer-discretionary complex.

## Fed, Rates & Credit

The bond market took the hot PCE in stride, which is itself informative. The 2-year Treasury yield ended around 4.07% and the 10-year near 4.38%, leaving the 2s10s spread at roughly +27 basis points — a modestly positive, gently steepening curve. Yields were little changed on the day because the inflation data, while high, matched expectations; the surprise that would have spiked the front end (a hot *core monthly* number) did not materialize.

But the rate-expectations picture has hardened over the week. CME FedWatch shows the July meeting pricing roughly a 63% probability of a hold and ~37% of a 25-basis-point hike, with later updates drifting toward ~70% hold as the in-line print cooled the most aggressive bets. By September the market leans clearly toward tightening: roughly 30% hold, ~51% one 25bp hike, and ~20% two hikes cumulatively. In other words, traders now assign a better-than-even chance that the next move is *up*, not down — an extraordinary repricing from the cut-heavy consensus that prevailed earlier in the cycle, and the direct legacy of the Warsh pivot plus a 4-handle on PCE. Bank of America's mid-week "rate hike" note helped catalyze the shift.

For credit and equities the implication is straightforward. A Fed that is plausibly hiking into a strong economy keeps the discount rate elevated and caps multiple expansion, especially for the long-duration cash flows embedded in AI and secular-growth stocks. It simultaneously supports financials (Friday's +0.37%) and value. The fact that 10-year yields are sitting at a relatively contained 4.38% — rather than 4.6%+ — is the only thing standing between "rotation" and "correction." Watch the 2-year: drift toward 4.20%+ and the hike gets priced harder; back toward 3.90% and the growth-scare bid is winning.

## Geopolitical & Policy

The Strait of Hormuz is back in the headlines, but — crucially — not in the oil price. Iran reportedly struck a cargo vessel near the Strait/Gulf of Oman on Thursday, and President Trump accused Tehran of a "foolish violation" of the ceasefire, while the IRGC warned commercial ships to use only Tehran-approved routes. Hezbollah's leader said the group will not disarm. Yet WTI fell 3.8% to $69.20 and Brent dropped 3.7% to $72.47 (its lowest since February 27).

The transmission mechanism is the story: despite the saber-rattling, tankers kept transiting the Strait largely unimpeded, and the US-Iran memorandum of understanding signed earlier in June continues to anchor a 60-day negotiating window, with Pakistani and Qatari mediators signaling that direct talks resume next week. The market is pricing *normalization*, not escalation — the war premium that briefly took crude into the $80s in mid-June has now almost entirely bled out. This matters mechanically for inflation: cheaper energy is the most direct lever to pull May's hot PCE lower in June and July, and it is doing the disinflationary work that a hawkish Fed would otherwise have to force through demand. Oil remains the single most important real-time variable for the rate path.

Elsewhere: Volkswagen is reportedly planning up to 100,000 job cuts and the closure of four German plants amid Chinese EV competition — a reminder that the global industrial cycle outside the US is far weaker than the American consumer would suggest.

## Focus Deep Dive — The AI Complex: Is the Trade Cracking, or Just Crowded?

The week's central question is whether June 2026 marks the top of the AI trade or merely a violent rotation within an intact bull market. The evidence points to the latter — for now — but the risk/reward inside the most expensive names has clearly deteriorated.

### Price Action & Technicals (Nasdaq Composite / NVDA proxy)

- **Nasdaq Composite levels.** The index closed 25,297.62 after a −4.48% week and five straight down days. The round-number psychological floor is **25,000**; a decisive break below it would open air toward the **24,300–24,500** zone (the early-June consolidation shelf). Overhead, **25,800–26,000** is now resistance — the level the index sliced through on the way down. A fifth consecutive decline that nonetheless held above 25,000 on heavy reconstitution volume is a constructive divergence: sellers had every excuse and could not force a breakdown.
- **NVDA support/resistance.** With NVDA at $192.53, the **$190** level (round number, prior breakout) is immediate support; below it, **$180** and then the 100-day region near **$172–175**. Resistance sits at the **$200** psychological line and then the **$210–215** gap from the early-June selloff, with the May 14 high of **$235.47** the bull target. At ~18% off the high, NVDA is in a normal-to-deep pullback, not a breakdown.
- **Bollinger Bands.** After five down days the major tech indices are pressing or piercing their lower daily bands, a short-term oversold condition that frequently precedes a relief bounce — but in a *downtrending* band regime (bands widening, price riding the lower rail), oversold can stay oversold. The signal to watch is a close back inside and above the 20-day mid-band.
- **MACD.** The daily MACD on the Nasdaq has rolled into a bearish crossover with a widening negative histogram — momentum is still deteriorating, not yet bottoming. A bullish divergence (price lower, MACD higher) has not formed.
- **Volume.** Friday was a record-volume session on the Nasdaq Closing Cross owing to the annual Russell reconstitution. That is mechanical flow, not conviction selling — it muddies the read, but the absence of a price breakdown on the year's heaviest tape is, if anything, mildly bullish.
- **Options positioning.** With the VIX at 18.38 — actually *down* on the day and well off the week's fear highs — the options market is not pricing panic. That cuts both ways: there is no capitulation/wash-out signal yet (which often marks tradable bottoms), but neither is there forced-deleveraging stress. Dealer gamma around the S&P 7,350–7,400 strike cluster likely contributed to Friday's pinned, low-range close.

### Fundamental Context

The bear case is now articulable: (1) AI monetization lags the capex — hyperscaler spending is real, end-customer ROI is murkier; (2) the buildout's costs are leaking into the broader economy, evidenced by Apple and Microsoft raising hardware prices to offset memory inflation, which both pressures their margins and feeds the very PCE print that threatens the Fed; (3) the IPO window is the canary — OpenAI postponing to 2027 and SpaceX round-tripping to its offer price suggest the primary market has lost its appetite to absorb trillion-dollar AI valuations. The bull case is equally concrete: Micron just printed 84.9% gross margins and guided to $50B in quarterly revenue, hard proof that demand and pricing are not slowing. Both can be true. The reconciliation is that the *fundamentals* of the AI hardware layer are accelerating while the *multiple* the market will pay for them is compressing — exactly what you'd expect when the discount rate is rising. That is a valuation correction inside a fundamental uptrend, and it favors owning the cash-generative picks-and-shovels (memory, power) on weakness over the pre-revenue, narrative-priced names.
