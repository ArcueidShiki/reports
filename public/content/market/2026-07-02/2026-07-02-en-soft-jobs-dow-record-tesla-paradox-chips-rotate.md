## Market Narrative

Thursday delivered one of the most instructive sessions of the year — not because the tape moved a lot, but because of *how* it moved. The headline June jobs report badly undershot expectations: nonfarm payrolls rose just **57,000** against a **115,000** consensus, and May was revised down to **+129,000**. In an ordinary cycle, a labor-market whiff like that would sink equities. Instead, in this cycle — one defined by a hawkish Federal Reserve under Chair Kevin Warsh openly weighing a *rate hike* to finish off 4%-plus inflation — a soft payrolls number is a relief valve. It made a July hike very hard to justify, and the market's response was to send the **Dow Jones Industrial Average up 1.14% to a fresh record 52,900.07**, its strongest single day in weeks.

But the relief did not reach everyone. The rally was overwhelmingly a value-and-cyclicals affair. The **S&P 500 finished essentially flat** (about −0.08%, just shy of its own record), and the **Nasdaq Composite slipped** roughly 0.6%, weighed by two idiosyncratic drags that had nothing to do with the Fed. The first was **Tesla**, which cratered about **7%** — its worst day in nearly a year — *despite* reporting blowout second-quarter deliveries. The second was a continuation of the semiconductor rotation that has dominated late June: after Wednesday's brutal chip session (the PHLX Semiconductor Index fell 6.3%, Micron −10.6%, Corning −13.6%), AI hardware kept bleeding as investors extended their profit-taking in the trade that carried the first half. The result was a mirror image of June 30, when records were printed almost entirely on the *chip* leg; on July 2, the record was printed almost entirely *without* it.

That divergence is the whole story. Breadth, not the index level, is where the information lived. If you owned banks, industrials, healthcare and the Dow's roster of old-economy multinationals, you had a banner day. If you owned the Magnificent Seven — minus Apple — you lagged badly. **Apple was the honorable exception among mega-caps, jumping about 4.8% to roughly $308.63** and single-handedly contributing a chunk of the Dow's record because it is one of the few large tech names inside that price-weighted index. Meanwhile Tesla and the chip complex, which sit in the Nasdaq and S&P but not the Dow, dragged the growth benchmarks lower. One tape, two completely different days depending on what you owned.

Underneath it all, the bond market did exactly what the equity rotation implied. Short-dated yields fell — the 2-year eased to about **4.17%** — as traders pushed the Fed's next possible move further out and trimmed the odds of a near-term hike. Gold firmed back above **$4,100**, oil slid further toward pre-conflict levels on continued U.S.–Iran de-escalation, and the VIX ticked up toward **17** even as the major indexes barely budged — a small tell that beneath the calm surface, positioning is nervous heading into a three-day weekend and the July FOMC. Records on one narrow leg, a marquee growth name in free-fall, and a "bad-news-is-good-news" macro inversion: this was a tape worth studying.

## Macro & Economic Data

**June nonfarm payrolls: +57,000 vs. +115,000 expected (prior revised to +129,000 from May).** A clear downside miss and the softest headline in months, running below even the subdued ~36,000 trailing 12-month average. Job gains were narrow: professional & business services +36,000, social assistance +25,000, healthcare +22,000 — while **leisure and hospitality shed 61,000**, which the BLS attributed to slower-than-usual seasonal hiring. This is a labor market decelerating, not collapsing, but decelerating faster than the Street modeled.

**Unemployment rate: 4.2% vs. 4.3% expected — but read the fine print.** The rate fell 11 basis points to a 12-month low, which *looks* like strength. It was not. The decline was driven by the **labor-force participation rate dropping 0.3 percentage point to 61.5%, the lowest since March 2021** — people leaving the workforce, not people finding jobs. A falling unemployment rate produced by a shrinking labor force is a statistical mirage, and decomposing it is essential before treating 4.2% as a hawkish data point.

**Wednesday's supporting data (context).** ADP private payrolls rose just **98,000** in June (below the 110,000 consensus, down from 122,000), the ISM Manufacturing PMI slipped to **53.3** from 54.0 (still expansionary but cooling), and — importantly for the disinflation case — euro-area CPI undershot at **2.8%** year-over-year versus 3.0% expected. Together with the June payrolls miss, the data mosaic tilts toward "slowing labor, cresting inflation" rather than the "no-landing, sticky-inflation" regime that dominated the spring.

Tesla delivered **480,126 vehicles**, obliterating a Bloomberg consensus near 397,000 and rising 25% year-over-year off a weak 2025 comparison. On the surface, a decisive beat. Yet the stock fell about 7%, and the reasons are a textbook lesson in reading *beneath* a headline. First, **Tesla produced only 451,758 vehicles while delivering 480,126** — it handed over roughly 28,000 more cars than it built, meaning the quarter was flattered by drawing down existing inventory rather than by fresh, organic demand. That is good for near-term cash flow but raises the question of what the run-rate looks like once the backlog clears. Second, deliveries say nothing about **margins**, which Tesla will not reveal until its **July 22** earnings call; with price cuts and incentives still in the mix, investors chose to fade a volume beat that carries unknown profitability. Third, the print landed after a strong run into the number — a classic "sell-the-news." Rivian's contrasting +8% (volume beat *plus* a raised outlook) underscores the distinction: the market rewards forward confidence, not just a backward-looking unit count.

## Fed, Rates & Credit

The June payrolls miss did real work on the rate curve. Heading into the print, the market — remarkably — had been pricing a meaningful chance of a *hike*, with CME FedWatch through the prior session showing roughly a coin-flip for a September 25-bp increase and only a minority expecting a hold. The soft +57,000 made a July hike "difficult to envision," in the words of BMO's rates desk, and short yields fell accordingly: the **2-year eased to ~4.17%** (still, notably, above the top of the Fed's target range), the **10-year slipped ~1 bp to ~4.46%**, and the curve held a modest ~+29 bp 2s10s slope. Chair **Kevin Warsh** kept the hawkish door ajar, reiterating the Fed's commitment to "restoring price stability" and promising a "good family fight" at the July FOMC. The framing for the next month is unusual and worth internalizing: this is not a cut-vs-hold debate, it is a **hike-vs-hold** debate, and Thursday's data shifted the odds toward *hold* without putting cuts anywhere on the table. Credit stayed calm; the small uptick in the VIX to ~17 was positioning insurance into a long weekend, not stress.

## Geopolitical & Policy

The dominant geopolitical thread remains the **U.S.–Iran de-escalation**, and its transmission runs straight through oil into inflation. Continued progress in indirect talks, recovering Strait of Hormuz flows, and ongoing strategic-reserve releases pushed **Brent toward $70 and pre-war levels**, extending crude's slide. ## Focus Deep Dive #2 — The Jobs Paradox and the Fed's Inverted Reaction Function (Macro)

The single most important idea for positioning right now is that **the sign on "good news" has flipped.** Because Warsh's Fed is fighting 4%-plus inflation and openly contemplating a hike, the market *wants* to see the economy cool. That is why a −58,000 miss on payrolls produced a *record* Dow. But the paradox has a second layer that most one-line takes missed: the "reassuring" **4.2% unemployment rate was manufactured by people leaving the labor force** (participation to 61.5%, a 4-year low), not by hiring. So the report is soft in a way that helps the *rate* narrative (fewer wage pressures, less need to hike) while quietly flagging *growth* risk (falling participation, −61k in leisure/hospitality, a +57k that is barely above stall speed). The clean interpretation: Thursday bought the bulls time by taking the July hike off the table, but it did so by revealing an economy that is decelerating — which is a tailwind only for as long as "slowing" doesn't tip into "cracking." The referee for that distinction is the next payrolls report and the July FOMC.
